RMT record keeping requirements in Ontario

If you practise massage therapy in Ontario, your charting isn't only a habit, it's set out in a regulation and a College standard. Here's what those two documents actually ask for, in plain English, with a checklist you can print and run against your own files.

This page summarizes the rules as published on the dates below. It isn't legal advice, and it doesn't replace reading the source. Rules change, so check the current versions linked at the end before you rely on any detail.

Where the rules come from

Two documents do most of the work:

  • Ontario Regulation 544/94 (the General Regulation under the Massage Therapy Act, 1991). Part III, “Records” lists what you have to keep. CMTO calls this the Records Regulation.
  • CMTO's Standard of Practice: Record Keeping, last updated September 8, 2026. It points back to Part III, to the Personal Health Information Protection Act, 2004 (PHIPA), and to the record-keeping items in the Professional Misconduct part of the same regulation.

Section 7 of the regulation also says you have to check, “at reasonable intervals,” that your records meet Part III. The standard repeats it: keep records compliant and verify regularly. The checklist at the bottom of this page is one way to do that.

The four records you keep

Part III and the CMTO standard name four kinds of records, not one:

  1. Daily appointment record (s. 8). The name of each client you examine, treat or serve, and the time of the appointment. Your booking calendar may already be this, as long as you can keep it.
  2. Equipment service record (s. 9). When equipment used with clients was serviced. For a solo RMT this might be a short log for a hydraulic table or a hot stone heater.
  3. Financial record for each client (s. 10). What treatment was given, the fee charged, and a copy or record of the receipt issued. If a payment app issues your receipts, make sure you can still get a copy of each one per client.
  4. Client health record for each client (s. 11). The chart itself, covered next.

What goes in a client health record

Section 11(2) lists thirteen items. Paraphrased, the record has to include:

  1. The client's name and address.
  2. The date, time and duration of each visit.
  3. The name and address of the client's primary care physician and any referring health professional.
  4. Relevant medical history and a history of massage therapy.
  5. Details of every examination you performed, and every clinical finding and assessment you made.
  6. Every written report you received about examinations, tests, consultations or treatments done by someone else.
  7. Details of all advice you gave.
  8. Details of every referral you made to another health professional.
  9. Details of every fee or other amount you charged.
  10. A copy of every written consent.
  11. A copy of every needs assessment.
  12. A copy of any treatment plan.
  13. What treatment was applied at each visit, and the name of the RMT who applied it.

A few of these are easy to miss in a quick SOAP note:

  • Duration and time, not just date. “Nov 10, 10:00, 60 min” rather than “Nov 10.”
  • Advice. If you suggest heat, a stretch, or seeing their doctor, it belongs in the note. A plan line such as “Advised cl to try heat 10 min evenings; suggested she mention jaw clenching to her dentist” covers it.
  • The physician's name and address. Collect it on the intake form, and update it when it changes.
  • Reports from others. If a client hands you a physiotherapist's letter, a copy goes in their record.

Section 11(3) adds that every part of the record has to carry something that identifies the client or the record, such as the client's name or a file number on each page.

Every entry: dated, with an author

Section 11(4): every entry must be dated, and it must be possible to tell who made it. For a solo RMT that sounds automatic, but it matters in two places:

  • Late entries and corrections. If you add to a note the next day, the addition gets its own date. The regulation doesn't spell out a correction method, but a common, defensible practice is to never erase: strike through on paper so the original stays readable, or keep the earlier version in an electronic system, then date the change.
  • Shared clinics. If more than one person writes in a chart, initials or a name on each entry make the author clear.

The CMTO standard also says plainly: don't falsify records. A visible correction with a date is the opposite of that. A silent overwrite can look like it.

How long to keep records

Section 11(5) sets the minimum for client health records: at least 10 years after the client's last visit. If the client was under 18 at their last visit, it's at least 10 years after the day they turned or would turn 18. The CMTO standard says the same and adds that destruction must happen only after that period, in a confidential way (s. 11(6)).

In practice: a client you last saw in November 2026 means keeping that chart until at least November 2036. A 15-year-old last seen in 2026 who turns 18 in 2029 means at least 2039.

Ten years is long enough that the storage method matters. Paper fades and gets damp in basements. Devices get replaced. Whatever you use, have a plan to move records forward when you change phones, apps or offices.

Paper or electronic

Section 12 allows records to be kept electronically. The condition is that the system is designed and operated so records are secure from loss, tampering, interference, or unauthorized use or access. CMTO's standard uses the same words for all records.

The regulation doesn't name a specific feature. Questions worth asking about any system you use:

  • Loss: Is there a backup outside the one device? Could you restore it if the phone were lost tomorrow?
  • Tampering: If a note is changed later, can you show what it said before and when it changed? An edit history does this.
  • Unauthorized access: Is the device locked with a passcode? Who else uses it?
  • Ten years: Can you export every record in a format you could still open if the app disappeared, such as PDF or CSV?

Copies, leaving a clinic, closing

  • Copies on request. Under s. 13, when the client, someone they authorize in writing, or their legal representative after death asks in writing, you provide copies from the record you're primarily responsible for. You may wait for a reasonable fee to be paid first.
  • Who is the custodian? The CMTO standard asks you to know whether you're a health information custodian or an agent in each place you work, and to have that in your contract.
  • Leaving or closing. Transfer records when you retire, move, or a client asks. Tell clients what happens to their records. Never sell records as a practice asset.

Printable self-check

Pull three random client files and run them through this list. Print the page for a paper copy.

  • Name and address on the record, and an identifier on every page
  • Each visit has date, start time and duration
  • Physician (and any referrer) name and address recorded
  • Medical history and massage history present and up to date
  • Assessment findings recorded for each visit
  • Treatment applied each visit, with the RMT's name
  • Advice given is written down
  • Referrals written down
  • Written consent, needs assessment and treatment plan copies on file
  • Reports received from other providers filed
  • Fee for each visit recorded, and a copy or record of each receipt
  • Every entry dated, author identifiable; corrections visible and dated
  • Daily appointment record kept
  • Equipment service log kept
  • Backup exists off the main device; device is locked
  • No chart destroyed before 10 years after the last visit (or age 18 + 10)
