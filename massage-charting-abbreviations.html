Massage therapy abbreviations for SOAP notes

Abbreviations are how you chart a full session in the five minutes before the next client. They only help if anyone reading the note, including you a year from now, reads them the same way. Here are the ones massage therapists use most, grouped the way you write a note, plus the ones worth spelling out.

There's no single official list for massage therapy. Schools, clinics and insurers each use slightly different shorthand, so treat this as a common starting point and write down your own version (see the last section). Press print for a one-page-ish cheat sheet.

Abbreviations to spell out

Some short forms mean two or three different things depending on who reads them. In a massage note the risk is usually confusion rather than danger, but confusion is exactly what you don't want if a note is ever reviewed. Spell these out:

  • PT can mean patient, physical therapy or physical therapist. Write “client” and “physical therapist.”
  • DC or D/C can mean discontinue, discharge or Doctor of Chiropractic. Write “chiropractor” or “stopped.”
  • MS can mean musculoskeletal or a specific medical condition. Write out what you mean.
  • SI means sacroiliac in your world but something very different in a medical record. Write “sacroiliac area” if the note may leave your practice.
  • HA (headache) and HS (hamstrings, or a dosing term to others) are easy to misread. Spell them out.
  • WNL (within normal limits) suggests a clinical test you probably didn't do. Describe what you felt instead.
  • Home-made shorthand like “tite” or a smiley face. Fine in your head, unreadable to anyone else.

Also spell out anything that records a precaution or a referral, such as “avoided deep pressure on L calf, client reports varicose veins” or “advised client to contact her OB.” Those are the lines that matter most if a session is questioned, so they should be impossible to misread.

Same note, two ways

Too abbreviated: Cl c/o HA 3x/wk, TTP SCM, UT, HT SI. Tx NMT, MFR. WNL post. DC f/u.

Readable: Cl c/o headaches ~3×/wk, late afternoons. TTP B/L SCM and UT; HT R sacroiliac area. Tx: NMT UT, MFR suboccipitals. Cl reports headache 3/10 → 1/10. f/u 1 wk; cl to tell her chiropractor about the jaw clenching.

The second version is barely longer. It keeps the efficient shorthand (TTP, UT, Tx, f/u) and spells out the three items that could be read two ways.

Keep your own key

The simplest habit that makes abbreviations safe is a one-page key of the ones you use, kept with your records. If a note is ever requested by a client, an insurer or a board, the key lets someone else read it the way you meant it.

  1. Start from this page. Print it and cross out the ones you never use.
  2. One meaning per abbreviation. If TP means trigger point, don't also use it for “trapezius.”
  3. Date your key and keep old versions with the records written under them.
  4. Don't abbreviate the client's words. Quotes like “burning” or “like a band” go in as said.
  5. Check your requirements. Some insurers, employers and state boards have their own documentation expectations. Look at your board's rules and your liability policy, or ask them directly, rather than assuming.
